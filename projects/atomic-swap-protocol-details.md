## Claim 1: "Quote phase as step 0" — **CONTRADICTED / IMPLEMENTATION CONVENTION**

The appendix shows a step 0 *"Quote and joint-key setup"* where Bob sends Alice a *"Signed quote (price, expiry, refund pubkeys)"* before joint-key setup begins.

**What the papers actually say**:
- Gugger 2020 (Section 2 "Scenario"): *"We assume that they already have negotiated the price in advance (i.e. amount of bitcoin for amount of monero to swap.) This negotiation can also be integrated into the protocol, for example by swap services who provide a price to their customers."* [Gugger 2020 p.1] — i.e., **price agreement is explicitly off-protocol**. The integration into a quote step is named as an implementation option for "swap services" (makers), not as a protocol step.
- Hoenisch–del Pino 2021 (Section 3.1 "Situation"): *"Alice and Bob have agreed to a trade in which Alice will send amt_xmr to Bob, and Bob will send amt_btc to Alice. They require this exchange to be atomic, i.e. the change of ownership of one asset should effectively imply the change of ownership of the other."* [Hoenisch–del Pino p.3] — again, **price agreement is a precondition, not a step of the protocol**.
- The first formal step in both papers is **key generation** / DLEQ proof exchange. There is no separate "quote" message in either protocol description.

**What the implementation does**: In `xmr-btc-swap` / `eigenwallet/core`, makers do publish a *quote* over libp2p before swap setup begins. The maker `asb` computes its price from off-chain ticker data (Kraken, Bitfinex, KuCoin, optionally Exolix; see [[projects/eigenwallet]] for the v3.5.0+ release-note source). This is an implementation convention by the maker software — exactly the "swap services who provide a price to their customers" use case that Gugger anticipates. The quote is **not cryptographically required**; the swap construction works equally well between two peers who agreed on price out-of-band (CLI / matrix room / phone call).

**Is the quote signed?**: The `asb` quote response is exchanged inside the libp2p connection (which has authenticated transport via the noise / libp2p secure-channel framework), not a separate explicit signature on the quote payload. There is no Bitcoin / Monero signature on the quote contents. Therefore the appendix's *"Signed quote (price, expiry, refund pubkeys)"* should be reframed as *"Quote (price, min/max amounts) exchanged over libp2p; refund pubkeys are exchanged in the subsequent key-generation phase, not in the quote"*.

**Recommended appendix fix**: drop step 0; mention quote exchange in a footnote as an implementation-layer convention used by makers (e.g., eigenwallet/core), distinct from the cryptographic protocol.

## Claim 2: "BTC locks first by construction of the adaptor-signature primitive" — **PARTIALLY CONTRADICTED**

The appendix asserts BTC must be locked first because *"the act of broadcasting that adapted signature on Bitcoin publishes `s` as part of the on-chain transaction"* and that *"reversing the order does not compose"*.

**What the papers actually say**:

- **Order of operations (current `xmr-btc-swap` / `eigenwallet` direction, BTC→XMR)**: Hoenisch–del Pino 2021 Section 3.2 "Happy path": *"Bob locks up amt_btc in a Point Time Locked Contract (PTLC) locked using point S_a^btc by publishing tx_lock^btc. [..] Alice subsequently locks up amt_xmr in a shared output with public spend key S_a^xmr + S_b^xmr and public view key V_a + V_b by publishing tx_lock^xmr."* [Hoenisch–del Pino p.3]. So in this paper, **BTC locks first**, XMR second.
- **Same authors then explicitly say the opposite order is also feasible.** Section 4 "XMR to BTC atomic swaps", p.10: *"That protocol is appropriate for a use case in which the Service Provider (SP) is in the role of Alice, i.e. she offers buying XMR for BTC to her customers. [..] However, using that protocol to swap in the opposite direction is not feasible i.e. an SP should not offer buying BTC for XMR. The problem is that an SP (in the role of Bob) could be easily attacked: the taker (in the role of Bob) could agree on a trade with the SP, make him lock up funds on Bitcoin and then bail out at no cost. The SP could always refund his locked up BTC after some time, but he would have to pay for transaction fees to do so. The taker's ability to make the SP incur in transaction fees without penalty would expose the SP to running out of funds over time, which is why we refer to this as a draining attack. We need a different protocol to allow an SP to offer BTC/XMR buy trades, since the original makes it a hard requirement for the party holding BTC to move first. In the following sections we propose a new protocol which instead requires the party holding XMR to move first. This depends on the development of adaptor signatures based on Monero's ring signature scheme [..]"* [Hoenisch–del Pino pp.10-11].

  So Hoenisch–del Pino explicitly distinguish: (a) the **deployed COMIT protocol** has BTC-first by construction of *which adaptor-signature variant they use* (ECDSA-based on secp256k1) and *which party plays the maker* (Alice, the XMR seller, takes the BTC); (b) **the reverse direction is feasible but requires adaptor signatures on the Monero ring-signature scheme**, which was "work-in-progress" as of the 2021 paper.

- **Gugger 2020 has Alice (the XMR holder) move first** — the opposite direction from the deployed COMIT protocol. Gugger 2020 Section 4 "Protocol": *"Alice moves the monero into an address where each participant controls half of the private spend key (later referred to as key 'shares'). The Bitcoin scripting language is then used with semi-scriptless protocols to reveal one of the halves of the private spend key, depending on which participant claims the bitcoin."* [Gugger 2020 p.4]. **Gugger's protocol moves XMR first, then BTC.** Section 2.3 "Worst case scenario": *"If the monero are locked, Alice will be able to refund them if and only if Bob refunds his bitcoin first."* — same direction.

- **What the secret actually is**: In both papers, the secret is a **discrete-log-equivalent scalar** that is simultaneously a private-key share on Ed25519 and on secp256k1. Gugger 2020 Section 4.2: *"the Monero private spend key is split into two secret shares k_a^s and k_b^s [..] participants will not use any multi-signature protocol; instead, the private spend key shares are distributed during initialization of the swap process where one participant will gain knowledge of the full key k^s ≡ k_a^s + k_b^s (mod l) at the end of the protocol execution"* [Gugger 2020 p.2]. The cross-curve DLEQ proves the same scalar `k_a^s` corresponds to both an Ed25519 point (the Monero key share) and a secp256k1 point (the Bitcoin adaptor-signature decryption key). On-chain publication of the Bitcoin signature reveals the scalar, which is then usable as a Monero key share.

- **Which party's signature is the adaptor signature**: In the deployed COMIT direction (Hoenisch–del Pino, BTC→XMR), Bob (the BTC seller) sends Alice (the XMR seller, who locks XMR second and then claims BTC) an adaptor signature on the BTC redeem transaction encrypted under S_a^btc. Alice decrypts using s_a (her Monero key share) and publishes the decrypted Bitcoin signature, which reveals s_a on the Bitcoin chain. Bob then extracts s_a and uses s_a + s_b to spend the joint Monero. [Hoenisch–del Pino p.3, Fig. 4 p.9]

**Verdict**: the appendix's claim that "reversing the order does not compose" is **incorrect as stated**. The order CAN be reversed (Gugger 2020 has XMR-first; Hoenisch–del Pino 2021 Section 4 discusses an XMR-first variant); what is required to reverse it is the use of adaptor signatures on the Monero side (CLSAG-based, since Monero has no Bitcoin-style script), and the choice of which side moves first is driven by the **draining-attack analysis** (which party can be cost-attacked by a counterparty who walks away mid-swap, where "cost" is the on-chain transaction fee of the refund the locked-first party must pay). The deployed `xmr-btc-swap` / `eigenwallet` direction (BTC-first) reflects an economic constraint, not a cryptographic one.

**Recommended appendix fix**: replace *"reversing the order does not compose"* with *"The choice of which side locks first is driven by the draining-attack analysis (Hoenisch–del Pino 2021 §4): the party with the on-chain refund-fee burden must be willing to accept that cost. In the deployed BTC-first direction this is the BTC seller (Bob); the reverse XMR-first direction is feasible but requires adaptor signatures over Monero's CLSAG ring-signature scheme, which is implementable but not present in `xmr-btc-swap` / `eigenwallet`."*

## Claim 3: "Refund timelock asymmetry" — **CONTRADICTED (the framing is wrong)**

The appendix claims: *"The script-side refund (Alice's BTC) typically has a shorter timelock than the secret-side refund (Bob's XMR), so Alice cannot wait until Bob has refunded his XMR and then claim Alice-side BTC anyway."*

**What the protocols actually have**:
- **There is no XMR-side timelock at all.** Gugger 2020 Section 3.1 "Monero": *"Monero does not require any particular on-chain primitives (hashlocks, timelocks), all building blocks are off-chain primitives."* [Gugger 2020 p.2]. Monero has no script language to express a timelock, so XMR cannot have a "refund window". The XMR-side "refund" works by **one party publishing a Bitcoin transaction that reveals a secret**, which the other party can then use to spend the jointly-locked Monero out to themselves (and which key the secret unlocks depends on the protocol stage).
- **The timelocks are both on Bitcoin.** Hoenisch–del Pino Section 3.3 "Expiries" [p.5]: *"The value of the two timelocks t_1 and t_2 must be confirmed before Alice and Bob can sign any transactions. Timelock t_1 determines how long Alice will have to publish and confirm tx_lock^xmr, and safely redeem tx_lock^btc. Timelock t_2 determines how long Bob has to refund his bitcoin after tx_cancel^btc is published by either party."* So t_1 is the **cancel timelock** (when the cancel-refund path becomes available on Bitcoin) and t_2 is the **punish timelock** (after which Alice can punish offline Bob by taking both BTC and abandoning XMR).
- **Canonical mainnet values, upstream comit-network/xmr-btc-swap**: `bitcoin_cancel_timelock = 72` blocks (≈ 12 hours), `bitcoin_punish_timelock = 72` blocks (≈ 12 hours); total refund window ≈ 24 hours. Verified from [`swap/src/env.rs`](../sources/2026-05-22-github-comit-network-xmr-btc-swap-swap-env-rs.rs).
- **Canonical mainnet values, current eigenwallet/core**: `bitcoin_cancel_timelock = 24` blocks (≈ 4 hours), `bitcoin_punish_timelock = 144` blocks (≈ 24 hours). Verified from [`swap-env/src/env.rs`](../sources/2026-05-22-github-eigenwallet-core-swap-env-rs.rs). Total refund window 168 blocks ≈ 28 hours. Matches eigenwallet/core release notes for v4.0.0 (2026-03-16): *"Reduce cancel timelock to 24 blocks (4 hours). [..] the refund window ends after `4 + 24 = 28` hours instead of the previous `12 + 24 = 36` hours. The punish timelock, which determines the length of the refund window, remains at 72 blocks (24 hours)."* (Note the release-note arithmetic says 72 blocks for the punish, but the code on master shows 144 blocks. Either the code is ahead of the release note, or the release-note phrasing refers to a different code path. The repo's master HEAD on 2026-05-22 has 144.) The user should treat the current canonical value as **144 blocks (~24h) for `bitcoin_punish_timelock`** since the source code is the canonical reference.

**Recommended appendix fix**: replace the "BTC vs XMR timelock" framing with *"Both timelocks are on the Bitcoin chain. `t_1` is the cancel timelock (≈ 4h on eigenwallet mainnet, was 12h in upstream COMIT); after `t_1` blocks confirm `tx_lock^btc`, either party can publish `tx_cancel^btc`. `t_2` is the punish timelock (≈ 24h); after `t_2` blocks confirm `tx_cancel^btc`, Alice (the XMR seller) can publish `tx_punish^btc` to take Bob's BTC if Bob has stayed offline. Monero has no on-chain timelock primitive (Gugger 2020 §3.1); the XMR refund path works by leaking a secret on Bitcoin that the counterparty then uses to spend the joint Monero output."*

## Claim 4: "BTC-XMR atomic swap took ~4 years (2017 proposal to 2021 working implementation)" — **CONTRADICTED on the 2017 origin, VERIFIED on 2021 launch**

- **2017 origin claim is wrong.** TierNolan first proposed cross-chain atomic swaps on bitcointalk in 2013 (bitcointalk.org topic 193281, post #10, dated 2013-05-21; cited as reference [17] in Hoenisch–del Pino with URL ending in `?topic=193281`). Hoenisch–del Pino's own narrative date of "2015" appears to be wrong on its face — their cited URL is the 2013 post. The Decred-Litecoin first on-chain HTLC swap was 2017-09-19 ([Decred blog](https://blog.decred.org/2017/09/20/On-Chain-Atomic-Swaps/) :: accessed 2026-05-22 :: [archived](../sources/2026-05-22-blog-decred-org-2017-on-chain-atomic-swaps.html)).
- **The BTC-XMR specifically**: the **Gugger paper is dated 2020** (IACR ePrint 2020/1126). It is the first formal protocol for BTC-XMR. Hoenisch–del Pino's instantiation paper is 2021-01-27 (arXiv 2101.12332 v1; v2 2021-02-02). The COMIT public launch on getmonero.org is 2021-08-20.
- **Timeline corrected**: BTC-XMR atomic swaps went from **first proposed (Gugger 2020) → published protocol + reference code (Hoenisch–del Pino 2021-01) → public mainnet launch (COMIT 2021-08) → first community GUI release on mainnet (UnstoppableSwap, via Monero CCS milestones in 2022, see [[projects/eigenwallet]]) — about **18 months from first proposal to working mainnet implementation**, not four years.
- **What about the broader "4 years"?** A defensible weaker claim: from the **2017 Decred-LTC first on-chain HTLC** to the **2021 BTC-XMR first adaptor-signature swap** is about 4 years of cryptographic and engineering work to remove the HTLC requirement and bridge the secp256k1/Ed25519 curve gap. This is the **"why HTLC doesn't generalise"** story. So the appendix's 4-year claim is salvageable if reframed.
- **Primary technical blocker**: cross-curve DLEQ proofs (proving the same discrete log is held on two different elliptic curve groups). Gugger 2020 Section 3.3 introduces such a proof; Hoenisch–del Pino [Section 3.5 "Cross-chain DLEQ proof"] notes that this *"kind of proof can only be constructed for points on the same elliptic curve. The idea of proving discrete logarithm equality across different groups comes from [12]"* — i.e., the Noether 2018 MRL-0010 technical note. So the genuine cryptographic delay was waiting for cross-curve DLEQ proofs to be available, which arrived in 2018, plus implementation effort. Plus the ECDSA one-time VES primitive (Fournier 2019, ref [8] in Gugger).

**Recommended appendix fix**: change *"2017 proposal to 2021 working implementation via adaptor signatures over Ed25519 and secp256k1"* to *"BTC-XMR was the hardest case because Monero has no scripting language for HTLCs; the cryptographic gap (cross-curve DLEQ proofs + ECDSA one-time VES) was bridged by Noether 2018 and Fournier 2019, the protocol was published by Gugger in 2020, and the COMIT reference implementation reached mainnet in 2021-08, then community GUI in 2022. About four years from first on-chain HTLC swap (Decred-Litecoin, 2017-09-19) to first BTC-XMR mainnet adaptor-signature swap (COMIT, 2021-08-20)."*

## Claim 5: "Free-option problem (Han et al. 2019)" — **VERIFIED with caveat on the framing**

- The citation is real and the paper does the work claimed. Han, Lin, Yu, "On the optionality and fairness of Atomic Swaps", IACR 2019/896. Key quotes [Han 2019 p.1]:
  - *"In particular, we model the Atomic Swap as the American Call Option, and prove that an Atomic Swap is equivalent to an American Call Option without the premium. Thus, the Atomic Swap is unfair to the swap participant."*
  - *"We use the conventional Cox-Ross-Rubinstein option pricing model in Finance to estimate the premium, and show that the estimated premium for stocks and fiat currencies is approximately 0.3%."*
  - Section 1.1 (p.2): *"in the default timelock setting, the profit and the mitigated loss of our selected cryptocurrency pairs are approximately 1%, while for stocks and fiat currencies the values are approximately 0.3% and 0.15%, respectively."*
- **What the paper does NOT directly say** is that the free-option problem is "the structural reason atomic-swap volume has remained small". The paper notes that DEX market volume in 2019 was around 50,000 ETH with ~4,000 active traders across all DEXes ([Han 2019 p.1] citing ref [2]), but this is descriptive of market state, not a causal claim about the free-option. The paper's contribution is **the formal proof of equivalence to a premium-free American Call Option and the empirical premium estimation (≈ 2% for crypto, vs 0.3% for fiat)**, plus a proposal for a "fair atomic swap" protocol with a premium mechanism.
- **What can be reasonably claimed from this paper**: the atomic swap initiator gets a free option worth ~2% of the asset value, which is a structural cost to the counterparty, which is a plausible (and quantified) reason why makers find atomic-swap economics unattractive at scale. The stronger claim "this is *the* reason volume hasn't scaled" needs additional sources to support it — the paper provides the mechanism, not the empirical attribution.

**Recommended appendix fix**: keep the citation; soften the claim from *"the structural reason atomic-swap volume has remained small"* to *"Han et al. 2019 prove the atomic swap is equivalent to a premium-free American Call Option, and quantify the implicit option premium at approximately 2% of asset value for cryptocurrency pairs (vs ≈ 0.3% for stocks/fiat), which is a structural cost to the counterparty."*

## Claim 7: "HTLC vs adaptor-signature applicability" — **VERIFIED with refinement**

- The dichotomy in the appendix (*"HTLC for script-compatible pairs; adaptor signatures for pairs where one side has restricted scripting (e.g. Monero)"*) is correct **as a first cut**. Decred-Litecoin 2017-09-19 is the first on-chain HTLC swap, confirmed and archived above.
- **More precise statement of what's required for each**:
  - **HTLC**: both chains need a script that supports hashlocks (HASH160/SHA256 + OP_EQUAL) and timelocks (CHECKLOCKTIMEVERIFY or CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY). Gugger 2020 Section 3.2 cites SegWit's transaction-malleability fix as a Bitcoin-side requirement [Gugger 2020 p.2]: *"The bitcoin transactions in this protocol require the fix for transaction malleability provided by the SegWit upgrade. This allows us to chain transactions without necessarily broadcasting them. This protocol is only compatible with cryptocurrencies which use a bitcoin style UTXO model and have an equivalent malleability fix such as Litecoin (i.e. Bitcoin Cash is not compatible.)"* — so even for the "script-rich" side, the requirements are stricter than just "has a script language".
  - **Adaptor signatures**: at least one chain needs a script that can support a 2-of-2 multisig + relative timelock (Bitcoin's script suffices); the other chain just needs a signature scheme over which an adaptor variant exists. Gugger uses ECDSA one-time VES (Fournier 2019) on Bitcoin's secp256k1, and Ed25519 key-sharing on Monero. The cross-curve DLEQ proof is the gluing primitive.
  - **Are there pairs where neither works?** Yes: any chain pair where both chains have *neither* script support *nor* a usable adaptor-signature scheme is out of reach. In practice this is hypothetical — most chains have at least one of the two. Grin is an example of "no script", but Schnorr adaptor signatures still work (Gugger 2020 cites [4] for Grin-Bitcoin).
- **Decred-LTC 2017 date is verified** above.

**Recommended appendix fix**: small refinement — HTLC requires not just "scripts" but specifically hashlock + timelock + transaction-malleability fixes (Gugger 2020 §3.2). Adaptor signatures need a 2-of-2 + relative-timelock script on at least one side, plus an adaptor-signature primitive on the other side's signature scheme. Pairs where neither works are hypothetical.
